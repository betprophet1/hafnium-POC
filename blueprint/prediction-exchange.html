Prediction Exchange Blueprint

A build plan for a self-clearing CFTC venue running a central limit order book and an RFQ desk over binary, categorical, scalar and parlay contracts — on AWS, in Rust, with multiple sequencer groups from day one.

What changed in this revision

This revision settles how the venue behaves, not only how it is built. Every book is continuous — no auction or batch mode in any tier, because an order must not behave differently depending on how busy its market is (§6, §21). Prices are ticks and quantities are stakes: the engine works in tick ordinals backed by exact rationals, and the edge converts odds and stakes into contracts (§6). The order book is specified down to the individual order, including what happens when it is full, and the order types now cover event start, reduce-only and a spend cap (§6). Rate limits and money each have one home: flood limits at the gateway and rule limits in sequenced state (§12), and fake, sandbox or live money fixed per account (§16), with payment adapters tested from both sides (§14). New sections cover self-serve environments (§16), the running system and its interfaces (§18), cost (§19) and non-binary payoffs (§22); API compatibility and versioning are in §13. What is still open is listed at the end of §21.

Section 01What a prediction market changes

Most exchange designs are borrowed from equities or futures. Several properties of event contracts break those assumptions — some make the system dramatically simpler, some harder. Getting these right at the start is worth more than any later optimisation.

Section 02Instrument taxonomy and the RFQ desk

Not every market is a simple yes/no on a 1¢ ladder. Four instrument classes need to coexist. The good news is that all four are binary in payoff, so the collateral model of §7 survives intact; what changes is how instruments come into existence, how finely they tick, and whether they trade on a book or through a quote.

A parlay is still a binary — which saves you

A five-leg parlay pays $1 or nothing. The buyer risks the premium; the seller's worst case is $1 − premium. That means a parlay is fully collateralisable exactly like every other contract, and it needs no new clearing machinery. What it needs is different plumbing:

  • Instruments are created on demand. You cannot pre-list the combinations. The instrument ID is a canonical hash of the sorted leg set — (event_id, outcome_id, side) tuples in a defined order — so the same parlay requested by two customers resolves to the same instrument and can accumulate a book.
  • They live deep out of the money. A five-leg parlay might be worth 0.8¢, where a 1¢ tick is unusable. Parlays default to a 0.1¢ tick; the ladder generalises cleanly (§6).
  • They settle early, and that matters commercially. The moment any leg resolves against the parlay, the contract is worth zero — there is no need to wait for the remaining legs. Emitting that expiry immediately releases the seller's collateral, often weeks early. Maintain a leg→parlay index so a resolving event can find every parlay that references it.
  • Naive pricing is wrong. The product of marginal probabilities assumes independence, and parlay legs are usually correlated. The exchange should not price them. Responders should, which is exactly what RFQ is for.

Parlays break the "one event, one sequencer group" rule — here is the fix

§5 groups markets by event so all outcomes of an event stay on one core. A parlay spans several events, which may sit on different sequencer groups. The resolution is that a parlay does not need atomic interaction with its legs at trade time: it is collateralised standalone at $1 per contract, so its book, its matching and its clearing are entirely self-contained. Give parlays their own sequencer group (or a few). The only cross-group coupling is at settlement, where a resolving event emits sequenced expiry events into the parlay sequencer groups — and settlement is not latency-critical.

Netting a parlay against positions in its legs is a margin question, not a matching question, and it belongs in the risk service. That distinction is what keeps the matching engine simple, and it is the subject of §8.

Section 03Reference architecture

The boundary that shapes everything else is the protocol boundary. Every wire format — native binary, FIX, WebSocket, REST — is decoded into one canonical internal message at the edge, and re-encoded per protocol on the way out. Nothing past that boundary knows or cares which protocol a client speaks, which is what keeps the engine single-format and the adapters independently deployable.

The load-bearing decision

Everything downstream of the sequencer is a deterministic function of the journal. No wall-clock reads, no floating point, no map-iteration-order dependence, no allocation in the engine. That single constraint buys replay-based recovery, byte-identical hot standbys, a free backtest harness, and the ability to prove to a regulator what the engine did on any past day. §4 is how you actually hold the line, and §18 draws every running component and the interfaces between them.

Section 04Determinism and replay

Determinism is not a property you notice missing until an incident you cannot reproduce. It is bought by one rule, applied without exception: the engine is a pure function of the journal. Anything it needs to know arrives as a sequenced event; anything it observes about the world on its own is a bug.

Every source, and how it is closed

The discipline is an enumeration, not a principle. These are the leaks that occur in practice, in rough order of how often they turn out to be the culprit:

Time, specifically

Time is the leak that catches almost everyone, because it fails invisibly under load and obviously in a quiet market. If the engine advances its clock only when a message arrives, a GTD order in a market with no flow never expires — there is no event to move time forward.

The fix is a tick event: the sequencer injects a timestamped TIME record on a fixed cadence, journalled like any other. Expiry, quote lifespans and the MM auto-pull windows of §6 all fire off ticks. At 1 ms cadence that is 1 000 events per second of pure overhead — nothing against your throughput budget, and it buys millisecond expiry precision that replays exactly.

Keep the two kinds of timestamp apart

A timestamp used for logic — expiry, ordering, audit — comes from the sequencer and is replayed. A timestamp used for measurement — the NIC hardware clocks of §9 — is observational metadata that must sit outside the state machine and be excluded from golden comparison. Conflate the two and every replay diffs, at which point the apparatus is worthless and someone will quietly switch it off.

Enforcement, not discipline

None of this survives a growing team if it lives in a style guide. Make it structural, so a leak fails the build rather than an incident review:

// engine crate: no_std removes the ability to read a clock or touch a file
// at all — the strongest enforcement the language offers, worth its friction
#![no_std]

// clippy.toml — the leaks no_std does not already close
disallowed-types = [
  "std::collections::HashMap",     # randomised seed → unstable iteration
  "std::collections::HashSet",
]
disallowed-methods = [
  "std::time::Instant::now",
  "std::time::SystemTime::now",
  "rand::thread_rng",
]
  • A dependency allowlist in CI. The engine crate may depend on nothing that can read a clock, open a socket or allocate. A capability that is unreachable cannot be called by accident.
  • A CI grep for f32 and f64 in the engine tree — no lint covers primitive types cleanly, and integer cents make floats unnecessary anyway. The one place a float genuinely tempts is a fee proportional to p(1−p); compute it in fixed point, choose a rounding rule, and unit-test the boundary cases.
  • Reproducible builds: pinned toolchain, committed lockfile, explicit target-cpu (never native), no embedded paths or timestamps. Verify by building on two machines and comparing hashes.

Snapshots

Replay from genesis stops being practical within weeks, so state is checkpointed. Three rules make a snapshot trustworthy:

  • Taken by a replica, never the primary — the hot path never pauses to serialise itself.
  • Canonically encoded, so the same state always produces the same bytes: explicit field order, no padding. A snapshot that is not itself deterministic silently poisons every replay started from it.
  • Verified by construction: nightly, confirm that loading a snapshot at sequence N and replaying to M reaches byte-identical state to a replay from genesis to M.

Two honest caveats

Golden replay proves equivalence, not correctness. It tells you the new build behaves like the old one and says nothing about whether the old one was right. Pair it with property tests on the invariants of §14 — positions sum to zero, no order matches through its limit — which check truth rather than sameness.

Intentional behaviour changes will diff, by design. A new order type or a changed priority rule makes golden replay light up, and the tempting response — re-baseline and move on — destroys the value of the practice. Instead, gate behaviour on a protocol_version carried in the journal: old events replay under the rules that were live when they happened, new events run the new rules. Build this before the first behaviour change, not after. Retrofitting it means either orphaning your journal history or maintaining an engine fork per era, and both cost far more than the switch statement would have.

The rule, in one line

If the engine needs to know it, it arrives as a sequenced event. If the engine can observe it directly, that is the bug — and every practice in §14 is downstream of holding this line.

Section 05Sequencer availability and sequencer groups

The concern is right, but it contains two different questions wearing one word. Is the sequencer a single point of failure? — largely no, because a single logical sequencer is already a replicated group. Should there be more than one sequencer? — eventually yes, but for blast radius rather than throughput, and the way you make it work is to stop needing a global order at all.

"One sequencer" already means three machines

The sequencer is a role, not a box. It is implemented as a Raft group — three nodes in this design — with one leader assigning the sequence, followers accepting the replicated journal, and an election when the leader dies. Because nothing is acknowledged to a client before it has replicated to a majority (§9), a leader failure loses no accepted order — the new leader already holds every committed event and resumes from the last committed index. Tuned, with all members in one cluster placement group, failover runs from a few hundred milliseconds to a couple of seconds, dominated almost entirely by how aggressively you set the heartbeat timeout.

Expiry, tick events, audit order and surveillance all assume time never runs backwards; do not leave it to two clocks to agree.

The residual failure replication cannot fix

Determinism (§4) means every replica processes the same event the same way — including an event that crashes the engine. A malformed or pathological input that panics the leader will panic the newly elected leader too, and then the next one. This is the real single point of failure, and adding replicas does nothing for it. Defend it before the boundary instead: total validation at the gateway, an engine that returns a reject rather than panicking on anything it can express, a fuzzed decoder (§14), and a quarantine path that journals a repeatedly-fatal event as rejected and moves on, loudly. Design this in phase 2; discovering you need it during an incident is discovering it too late.

Why more than one is possible at all

The instinct is that an exchange needs one global total order. It does not. It needs a total order per unit of consistency, and in this venue that unit is the event, not the venue. Work through what could possibly require ordering across two unrelated markets:

  • Relative ordering of two orders in different markets — unobservable by anyone, and nothing in the matching rules refers to it.
  • An account's cash — genuinely global, and already solved: credit sleeves (§7) divide buying power between sequencer groups in advance, so the hot path never performs a cross-group transaction. The invariant is simply that the sum of an account's sleeves never exceeds its cash.
  • Categorical outcomes and set burns — confined to one event, and the event is the unit of grouping, so they never cross.
  • Self-match prevention and position limits — defined per market or per event. Local.

Nothing on the hot path needs a global order. That is the finding that makes multiple sequencer groups safe, and it is the same conclusion large venues reach when they spread symbols across independent matching segments, each with its own sequence space and its own market-data feed.

What multiple groups cost

  • Replay becomes N streams. Debugging a single market is unaffected — that market lives entirely in one sequencer group, and §4 works exactly as written. Reconstructing the venue needs a merge, and the merge rule must be fixed and deterministic so two people reconstructing the same day agree.
  • Clocks must be comparable, and on AWS they are good to tens of microseconds, not better. Each group stamps its own timestamps, so cross-group ordering is only as good as clock discipline. AWS exposes a PTP hardware clock from the Nitro System as a local device (/dev/ptp0) that chrony follows directly; AWS states a clock error bound under 40 µs, and ClockBound reports that bound alongside each reading. There is no grandmaster to run and no sub-microsecond skew to be had. Two events in different sequencer groups closer together than the error bound cannot be put in true order by any clock, so the merge rule below is a deterministic order rather than a claim about which happened first, and cross-market surveillance treats events inside the bound as simultaneous.
  • Capital sits idle in sleeves on sequencer groups a trader is not currently using. Dynamic resizing against observed usage recovers most of it.
  • Operational surface multiplies: N groups to monitor, N failovers to drill, N snapshot schedules, and an event-migration procedure you must actually rehearse.

Two writers on one stream — don't

The literal reading of the question is two nodes appending to the same log concurrently. It is possible: pre-assign slot ranges, so one owner takes even sequence numbers and another the odd, with a hole-filling protocol for when an owner stalls. It buys throughput, and it costs tail latency, because a slow or dead owner blocks the stream until a hole-fill timeout fires. That trade is exactly backwards for a venue whose product is a low p99. One leader per stream; split the venue across streams.

Aeron on AWS, and Aeron from Rust

Aeron is the right choice and it runs fine on AWS — Cluster uses UDP unicast, so the absence of multicast costs you nothing here (it costs you on market data instead, §9). The awkward part is the language. Aeron's consensus module is Java. The C and C++ offerings are clients; the Rust crates in the ecosystem are wrappers over the Aeron C API for transport, and the one Rust package that speaks the cluster client protocol is explicitly experimental. There is no production Rust implementation of the consensus module, so a pure-Rust Aeron Cluster is not available to you today.

Pin the version, and pin it to 1.53

1.53.0 released 26 August 2026; JDK 17 has been the minimum since 1.46. Its breaking changes — removal of Image.controlledPeek, the end of the -javaagent requirement for event logging, archive error codes surfaced to the C and C++ APIs — are upgrade pain for existing deployments and free on a greenfield build. One is a requirement rather than a convenience: unauthenticated ConnectRequest is no longer accepted, so a credentials supplier belongs in the cluster configuration from the first commit rather than as a retrofit. 1.52.0 also moved log buffer creation and removal off the conductor thread onto a background agent, which retires a jitter source that would otherwise bite at the instrument counts in §10. Real Logic backports fixes to older lines, so pinning does not strand you.

What the Java piece actually is

Worth being concrete, because “a JVM in the critical path” sounds larger than it is. Aeron Cluster is four processes, and only some of them have to be Java:

So the irreducible Java is the consensus module and the archive — code other venues already run in production — plus a single class implementing ClusteredService: roughly ten callbacks, a few hundred lines, and no business logic. It receives committed messages and forwards them over shared memory to the Rust engine.

What the container hands over is precisely the contract §4 asks for. Callbacks fire only after commit to the replicated log, in identical order on every node, carrying a cluster-supplied timestamp rather than a local clock read. The determinism envelope is inherited rather than built. The interface even encodes the boundary: doBackgroundWork(nowNs) is documented as the one place for non-deterministic work, and is forbidden from mutating service state.

The shim is small, but it sits on the critical path and it is where a subtle bug corrupts the regulatory record. Four things to get right:

  • Fence the snapshot behind the IPC queue. onTakeSnapshot hands the service a publication and expects state written into it — but the state lives in Rust. With fire-and-forget IPC the engine may be many messages behind when the request arrives, yielding a snapshot of a state the log never passed through. It is corrupt and silently so: nothing fails until a recovery replays from it. The request must be ordered behind every message already forwarded, whether by lock-step IPC or a sequence-numbered fence the engine acknowledges.
  • Egress is a second hop, and it is the one on the acknowledgement path. Ingress runs Java to Rust; execution reports return Rust to Java to ClientSession.offer(). The return leg sits on order acknowledgement, which is the latency customers actually feel. If the round trip disappoints, the escape is to let the engine publish market data directly on its own streams — the fan-out tier in §9 is separate anyway — while keeping execution reports on cluster egress, because whether an order filled is the last place to hand-roll delivery guarantees.
  • The pair shares fate. The JVM and the engine are one logical node. If the engine dies while the JVM lives, the node keeps acknowledging to the cluster while applying nothing — a divergence that surfaces only at the next snapshot or the next election. The service must fail-stop when its IPC peer is gone, and supervision must treat the two processes as a single unit.
  • Role has to reach the engine. Followers apply the same log but must not emit. onRoleChange has to be forwarded, or anything the engine publishes directly goes out three times over.

onNewLeadershipTermEvent carries an appVersion on the leadership term. That is Aeron's own application-version gate, and it maps cleanly onto the protocol_version mechanism in §4 — deploy-dark-then-activate has native support rather than needing to be layered over the top.

On tuning the JVM: Real Logic's code is written to allocate on startup and not in steady state, so the goal is that garbage collection never runs during a session, not that it runs quickly. Pre-touch the heap, use huge pages, pin the consensus module thread alongside the engine cores in §9, and warm the JIT before the node takes traffic. Since 1.53 the thread names are distinct per module (aeron-md-snd, aeron-md-rcv and the rest), which makes that pinning scriptable in the host configuration of §15 instead of a runbook step involving guesswork about process IDs.

The availability zone tension

On AWS this is the decision that actually costs you something. A Raft commit needs a majority, and cross-AZ round trips are roughly an order of magnitude slower than same-AZ ones. You cannot have both a fast commit and zero data loss on the loss of an availability zone.

LayoutCommit latencyLoss of one AZ
3 nodes, one AZ, cluster placement group recommendedFast — the whole quorum is same-AZVenue halts. Promote the async replica in a second AZ, accepting a documented, non-zero RPO.
3 nodes across 3 AZsEvery order pays a cross-AZ round trip, foreverSurvives with no data loss and no intervention.

Take the first row. An AZ failure is rare, a venue can halt for one, and the recovery is a rehearsed procedure with a published RPO and RTO. Paying a cross-AZ round trip on every order for the lifetime of the venue, to avoid an event that may never happen, is the worse trade — and it is a trade you can revisit once you have measured what cross-AZ actually costs you (§9).

Moving an event between sequencer groups

Event migration is a scheduled procedure, never a live one, and it must leave both journals independently replayable:

  1. Halt the event in sequencer group A — itself a sequenced event.
  2. Drain to quiescence, then take a canonical snapshot of that event's state.
  3. Write TRANSFER_OUT into A's journal and TRANSFER_IN, carrying the state, into B's.
  4. Bump the version of the group map and distribute it to the gateways.
  5. Resume in B.

Because the move appears in both journals, each sequencer group remains a pure function of its own journal across the boundary — the property from §4 survives event migration, which is the whole reason to record the transfer on both sides rather than just moving memory.

Recommendation

You have said to assume scale from day one, so: launch with several sequencer groups, not with a group key and N=1. Three groups of three nodes each — the shape §18 deploys and §19 prices — one AZ per group with an async replica elsewhere, acknowledging only after replication. Running multiple sequencer groups in production from the start is the only way the event-migration procedure, the cross-group settlement path and the merged audit view get exercised before they matter — a grouping scheme that has only ever run at N=1 is a design document, not a capability. Parlays get their own sequencer group from the outset (§2).

Add sequencer groups when blast radius justifies it, not when throughput does — one tuned group will carry far more than you are likely to see. And be honest about the limit: splitting contains state-dependent failures, not code bugs. The same poison pill runs in every sequencer group.

Section 06The matching engine

Because price is bounded, the order book stops being a data-structures problem. Nothing here needs a tree, a heap, or a hash map on the hot path.

This layout depends on the book never being crossed

One quantity, one FIFO queue and one occupancy bit per tick is correct only because a continuously matched book can never hold a bid and an ask at the same price — they would already have traded. Make that an invariant the engine asserts after every event rather than an assumption it relies on. The two masks are kept for a different reason: they make best bid and best ask one instruction each, with no cached best-price registers to keep correct through sweeps and cancels. Any future proposal for a call auction or batch window must also propose per-side level arrays, because this struct cannot represent a crossed book.

Mass cancel, idempotency and snapshots

  • Every order sits on two lists. Besides its tick's FIFO, each order record carries a second pair of links threading it onto its session's list of resting orders. Cancel-on-disconnect and a mass cancel by session walk exactly that session's orders; a mass cancel by account walks the account's sessions; by market or event, the books themselves, one occupied tick at a time. Without the second list, "cancel everything for this firm" searches the whole slab, at the moment a market maker most needs it to be fast.
  • Client order ID dedupe is sequenced state, not gateway state. A table in the sequencer group maps (account, client order ID) to the order's outcome for a published retention window. It has to live there because a resend after failover arrives on a new session and possibly a new gateway (§5): gateway memory would have forgotten, and a per-session key would never match. Client-chosen keys go through a keyed hash whose seed arrives in the journal (§4), so the table resists flooding and replays exactly. It sits beside the slab and is sized and limited the same way.
  • Slot numbers never leave the engine. Order IDs are assigned by the exchange, never derived from a slab slot, and a snapshot serialises each book by walking its ticks in price order and each queue in time order. A restored sequencer group may place every order in a different slot and still produce identical outputs, which is what lets the free list, the capacity ceiling and even the slab layout change between releases without breaking replay (§4).

When the slab is full

The slab and its id map are sized when a sequencer group starts and never grow while it runs: growing would allocate on the hot path, and a resize mid-session is one more thing replay would have to reproduce exactly. So a full slab is a condition the engine must handle as a sequenced outcome, decided identically on every replica — never by an external monitor, and never by quietly making room.

That is deliberately conservative. The rule is one sentence in the rulebook, the customer sees a clean rejection rather than a fill followed by a surprise, and the engine can answer the question from the per-tick totals and masks without walking a queue.

A full slab is a sizing failure, not a trading condition

Size generously, and make the limit movable. A production order record is roughly 64–80 bytes, so a million resting orders in a sequencer group is roughly 100 MB including its id map. Reserve and pre-touch a ceiling well above expected use at boot, and treat the active limit as a journalled setting beneath it: raising it is a sequenced operator command that replays, in the spirit of §15's deploy-dark-then-activate, not a restart during trading. Size the id map from the ceiling, never the active limit, and keep it at most half full — an open-addressed table that fills does not degrade, it stops terminating.

What actually fills a slab is abuse and accumulation, not load. Per-account resting-order caps per sequencer group (§12) stop one participant from exhausting a sequencer group shared by every market in it. A maximum lifetime for good-till-cancelled orders, and cancellation of resting orders at resolution, stop years of forgotten orders piling up in books nobody trades. When §10 pages a dormant book out, its resting orders leave the slab with it; a cancel or fill that touches such a book brings it back in within the same sequenced event.

Alert well before the limit, from outside the engine. The monitor pages a human; only the engine ever rejects.

Odds, ticks, and the price you can actually compute with

The paragraphs above assume price is a number of cents. On a venue that quotes odds it is not, and the gap is not cosmetic. The current ProphetX v4 trading API carries price as decimal odds — 2.50 — alongside a preformatted display_price string, while the customer-facing product shows American odds. Neither of those is a quantity the engine can do arithmetic on, and treating either as one puts a rounding error in the money path.

The reason is worth stating precisely, because it decides the data structure. American odds are exact rationals: −110 means risk 110 to win 100, so the implied probability is exactly 110/210 = 11/21. The decimal form of that is 21/11 = 1.909090…, which does not terminate, so the wire value 1.91 is already a rounding. Recover the probability from it and you get 0.523560 where the true figure is 0.523810 — a quarter of a basis point, systematic, and always in the same direction.

The loss is one-sided, which is what makes it easy to miss. Positive American odds always convert to an exact two-decimal figure, because +A is simply 1 + A/100. Negative odds are 1 + 100/A and terminate only when A divides a power of ten. So the prices that survive the round trip are the longshots, and the ones that do not are −105, −110, −115 and −120 — which is to say the favourites, where the volume is.

The edge resolves an incoming quote to an ordinal by exact string or fixed-point match against that table and rejects anything that is not a listed level. The engine then works only in ordinals, and no float and no repeating decimal ever crosses the boundary. This costs nothing: the book was only incidentally indexed by cents, and indexing it by tick ordinal leaves the flat array, the occupancy bitmap and the single clz exactly as they were.

Never sort a book by the American odds number

−200 is a 66.7% chance and +150 is a 40% chance, but as signed numbers −200 sorts below +150. The convention is not monotonic in probability: it runs from +∞ down to +100, then jumps to −100 and continues to −∞, and there is no representable value between +100 and −100 because both mean an even chance. A comparison written against the displayed number will produce a book that is silently ordered wrongly in the middle of its range, which is exactly where the liquidity is. Decimal odds are at least monotonic, though inverted against price. The tick ordinal is the only safe sort key, and it should be the only one that exists in the engine.

Stake on the wire, contracts in the journal

The second divergence is quantity. ProphetX's quantity is a stake — an amount of money at risk — where §7 holds a signed integer count of contracts that each pay $1.00. These are the same object seen from two ends: a stake S at decimal odds d buys S × d of return, which is S × d contracts at a price of 1/d each. $100 at 2.50 is 250 contracts at 40¢, and the two descriptions agree to the cent.

They agree to the cent only if the stake lands on the right increment. With the wire decimal held as an integer D = d × 100, a stake of S cents returns SD/100 cents, which is a whole number of cents exactly when the stake is a multiple of 100/gcd(D, 100). That is the stake step column above, and it is not an arbitrary venue rule — it is the smallest bet at that price which does not require someone to round. At −110 it is a dollar, which is why risk-11-to-win-10 is the shape every bettor already has in their head.

Accept stake at the edge, because that is how customers think and how the existing integrations are written. Convert to contracts against the tick's exact rational, enforce the step, and journal the contract count — the same rule §13 applies to dialects, applied to quantity. The alternative, journalling a stake and a float price, puts the conversion inside the replay boundary and makes every historical fill depend on the rounding behaviour of whatever parsed it.

Allocation — who gets filled

Allocation policy is per instrument, set at listing, and decides how an incoming order is distributed across resting orders at a price. What it never varies is when matching happens: every book matches continuously, in every activity tier. An order that crosses fills at once and one that does not rests. There is no auction mode and no batch window, so an order behaves the same on the busiest market on the venue as on one that has not traded all week (§10).

Normalisation

Every inbound order is reduced to a single canonical form before it reaches the book: (market, BUY|SELL, tick, qty), always denominated in YES.

  • BUY NO @ p → SELL YES @ (1 − p)
  • SELL NO @ p → BUY YES @ (1 − p)

This is why one book suffices, and it is the reason a resting YES bid at 40¢ and a resting NO bid at 60¢ find each other automatically — after reflection they are a crossing bid and offer at the same price. Minting falls out of the normalisation rather than needing special matching logic.

Reflection needs the complementary tick to exist, so every tick ladder must be closed under p → 1 − p. A cent ladder is closed by construction. An American odds ladder is closed when +A and −A are listed together, because +A implies a probability of 100/(100+A) and −A exactly its complement; list them in pairs, and reject at listing any ladder that is not closed.

Retail hazard worth designing around

In a thin book, a market order can sweep from 12¢ to 91¢. Prediction markets are unusually exposed to this because most books are cold and because a 1¢ price move is a real probability change. Event start is where customers get surprised

A bid left at 40¢ before kickoff that fills a minute after an opening touchdown is technically correct and indefensible to the customer. So resting orders are cancelled at event start by default, and keeping one in-play is an explicit choice made on the order. The app should show which of a customer's orders will cancel at the start before it happens, not explain it afterwards.

The start is a sequenced event, never a clock read inside the engine (§4): every replica cancels at the same position in the log. Games start early, and a venue that clears books on the scheduled time alone will sometimes clear them after the first play — so the trigger is the data vendor's start signal, with the scheduled time as a backstop. Until the oracle adapters arrive in phase 3 (§17), operators sequence the start from the console against the schedule, which makes it a staffing requirement for every live event on the calendar. In test environments the dummy oracle sends the start on command (§16).

No stop or trigger orders on event contracts

Neither of the large prediction markets offers them on event contracts: Polymarket has no stop or trigger type, and Kalshi documents stop-loss and take-profit only for its perpetual futures. The reason is structural rather than an oversight. An event contract gaps on news — a touchdown can move a price from 30¢ to 70¢ in one print — so a stop becomes a market order at exactly the moment its fill is worst, which is the plain market order this section already refuses to expose. If the perpetuals of §22 ship, stop-loss and take-profit ship with them, because that is the product where a mark price moves continuously. On event contracts, a price alert in the app gives a customer the information without handing their position to the book mid-gap.

Market maker protections

  • Self-match prevention with a configurable policy (cancel resting, cancel incoming, or cancel both). Non-optional — MM firms run multiple uncoordinated strategies.
  • Rate-based auto-pull. If a firm is filled for more than N contracts within T milliseconds in one event, cancel all of its quotes in that event. This is the single feature that most improves quoted spreads in illiquid markets. Offer it to firms as order groups too: tag orders with a group, set a contract limit over a rolling window, and when fills exceed it every resting order in the group is cancelled and new ones are refused until the firm resets the group. That is the shape Kalshi's order groups already use, so existing integrations port directly.
  • Quote lifespan and per-event notional caps, enforced in the engine, not the gateway.
  • Kill switches at session, account, market, event and venue level, reachable from an operator console and from the firm's own API.

Section 07Collateral, minting and the risk check

The matching engine only ever sees YES. The clearing layer decides whether a fill transfers existing contracts, creates new ones from collateral, or destroys a complete set and returns a dollar. Getting this matrix right is the difference between a clean ledger and an unreconcilable one.

Position representation

Hold one signed integer per account per market: positive is YES, negative is NO. A complete set never persists — the moment an account holds both, collapse it to $1.00 of cash. The pre-trade risk check

Because there is no leverage, sufficiency is a single expression, evaluated per order against the account's uncommitted cash:

uncovered = max(0, qty − contracts_already_held_on_the_side_being_sold)
required  = uncovered × (side == BUY ? p : 1 − p)
accept if required ≤ available_cash

Here p is the exact rational behind the order's tick (§6), never a displayed price, and because quantities are held to the tick's lot step, required is always a whole number of cents and the check needs no rounding. Resting orders reserve their required amount. That is the entire risk engine for binary markets — no scenario grid, no correlation matrix, no intraday margin call.

Parlays

A parlay collateralises exactly like any other binary: the buyer posts the premium, the seller posts $1 − premium, and the same signed-integer position model applies. No new clearing path is needed. Two behaviours are specific to it:

  • Early worthlessness. As soon as any leg resolves against the parlay the contract is worth zero. The settlement service emits an expiry event into the parlay's sequencer group immediately, releasing the seller's collateral rather than holding it until the last leg resolves. Over a book of multi-leg parlays this is a large and continuous capital release, and market makers will price it in.
  • No leg netting at trade time. The matching engine treats a parlay as a standalone instrument. Offsetting it against positions in its legs is a margin computation, performed by the risk service outside the hot path — see §8, which is what makes parlay quoting economic at all.

Categorical markets

An event with N mutually exclusive outcomes is listed as N binary books, all pinned to the same sequencer group so cross-outcome logic stays local. Two consequences worth building for:

  • Set burns. One contract of every outcome pays $1.00 whatever happens, so a complete set goes back as cash automatically after the fill that leaves an account holding it. There is no explicit basket mint or redeem: trading already creates contracts, arbitrage across the N books works by ordinary orders, and the capital a market maker would save by minting a basket comes from portfolio margining (§8).
  • Worst-case-state margin. Collateral for a portfolio inside one event is −mini(payout if outcome i wins), an O(N) scan you can update incrementally. A firm short every outcome but one is genuinely low-risk; charging it full notional per leg drives it off the venue. In a fully collateralised venue this scan is a release rather than a charge, which is why it ships on day one — see below.

Releasing collateral before resolution

“I bet on the game, then bet the other way, so why is my money tied up until Sunday night?” is the most common complaint a fully collateralised venue receives, and it deserves a mechanism rather than a policy. There are four ways an account can be offset. They do not all come back at the same moment, and the reason is structural.

The unifying object is a payout vector: for each account and each event, one signed integer per state of that event, holding what the account's positions in that event pay if that state occurs. A fill touches one state for mutually exclusive outcomes, so the update is O(1), and the number that matters is its minimum:

floor = min over states s of payout(account, event, s)

Because every position in a fully collateralised venue is a long — selling YES from flat makes you long NO, funded at mint — every payout is non-negative and this floor is never negative. There is nothing to charge. The number is always money the account is certain to receive, sitting in the collateral pool, and the only question is whether it is handed back now or in January. §8's max(0, −mins ...) is the same scan read with the opposite sign; it stays identically zero until §22's leverage products make a payout negative for the first time.

This is why in-event offsetting is not margining and should never have been scheduled as though it were. There is no correlation matrix, no scenario grid, no lookback and no model risk — it is arithmetic over a bounded state vector, exact rather than statistical, and it is the same computation the engine already performs to collapse a complete set. Releasing it is also capital-neutral: because the floor is paid in every state, handing it over leaves every remaining claim in the event backed exactly as before.

Burn and advance are not the same release

The line that decides what can ship on day one is not one event versus many. It is whether the release destroys contracts.

  • A burn is final. The positions cease to exist, open interest falls — and must be reported falling, since it is a regulatory figure — and nothing that happens afterwards can reprice what is no longer there. Complete sets, full baskets and all-NO holdings are all instances of one rule, and all of them are safe to sweep inside the fill.
  • An advance is not. Paying a parlay seller its floor against hedges in the legs leaves every contract outstanding. The arithmetic is still exact and the pool is still whole, but the account has been paid for a claim that has not yet been extinguished, and anything that reprices a leg afterwards was not in the state space when the money went out the door.
A void reverses the event, and calls back what was released

§11 settles a void by reversing every trade in the event: each account is paid the negation of the net cash its fills, baskets and set burns in that event paid it, and ends as if it had never traded. Releases are not held back for this. Cash freed by a burn or paid to a seller may already have been withdrawn, so a void can take back more than an account still holds and leave its available cash below zero; the account can then receive cash but spend nothing until it is funded. Voids are rare and customers are identified, so the venue carries the shortfall as a receivable rather than freezing collateral on every market in case one voids. Refunding each holder's cost instead is not an option: once a seller has been paid, it can owe more than the pool holds.

The listing rule stands. Within a set of books that share a state space, void the event, never a single book. A reversal works per event, because baskets and set burns move cash across every book in it at once, so a field where one outcome can be voided while its siblings resolve cannot be unwound book by book.

Two implementation notes

  • Automatic, not a primitive. Sweep the floor inside the fill, in the engine, as part of the same sequenced transition — so it is deterministic and replays (§4). There is no explicit basket call to fall back on, and none is needed: the complaint at the top of this subsection comes from retail, which would never find a redeem basket button, and market makers get the capital a basket would have saved them from portfolio margining (§8).
  • Bound the state space at listing time. Outcomes inside one event are not always mutually exclusive — Chiefs win, Chiefs win by 7+ and total over 45.5 overlap. The states are then the coarsest partition that every book in the event is a union of, and each book carries a bitmask over it. Cap that space at 64 states, one machine word, and refuse at listing time to add a book that would exceed it. Fixed size, no allocation, the same discipline as the ladder in §6.

One honest caveat on immediately. Released cash lands in the sleeve of the sequencer group that owns the event (§5 makes the event the unit of grouping, so a set never straddles two). It is spendable there at once and spendable elsewhere after the next sleeve top-up. That is seconds, not hours, but it is not zero — so show a customer their buying power per venue-side balance rather than presenting one number that is briefly wrong.

Three different things get called "margin" — only one is leverage

1. Offsetting within a risk group. A trader short every outcome of a five-way categorical has a bounded worst case, and charging full notional on each leg is simply wrong. This is not leverage and involves no credit extension. build day one

2. Portfolio margin across related instruments. A market maker who sold a parlay and holds offsetting positions in its legs is far less exposed than $1 − premium suggests. Still no credit extension — the collateral held always covers the true worst case. phase two

3. Leverage. Collateral below the worst-case loss, with the shortfall extended as credit. This is the one that turns you into a clearing house with a default waterfall. not recommended

Worst-case-state margin, and why parlays need it

For a set of instruments sharing an underlying event structure, the requirement is the worst outcome across the joint states that can actually occur:

requirement = max(0, −min over reachable joint states s of  payout(portfolio, s))

For one categorical event this is a scan over N outcomes. For a parlay on k legs plus positions in those legs it is a scan over the reachable joint states — at most 2^k, and k is small in practice, so exact enumeration is cheap and needs no correlation model or simulation. Positions are grouped into risk groups: sets of instruments sharing at least one underlying event. Groups stay small, they recompute incrementally, and the whole calculation stays exact rather than statistical.

This matters commercially rather than theoretically. Without it, a responder quoting a five-leg parlay posts the full $1 − premium with no credit for the hedges it is holding in the legs. The capital cost shows up directly in the spread it quotes you, or in a refusal to quote. Portfolio margining is the difference between a parlay desk that works and one that does not — which is why it is phase two and not phase four.

Where the risk calculation lives

Not in the matching engine. The hot-path check stays the O(1) sufficiency test of §7 against a credit sleeve. The risk service recomputes group requirements off the sequenced event stream and adjusts sleeve grants as positions change. The engine never evaluates a joint-state scan while an order is in flight; it reads a number that the risk service has already published as a sequenced event.

What you would have to build if you ever did offer leverage

Worth writing down once so the decision is made with open eyes, because each item is a team rather than a sprint: a margin methodology plus independent validation and back-testing; initial and variation margin flows with intraday calls; member capital and admission standards; a guaranty fund with exchange skin-in-the-game; a documented default waterfall; a default management process rehearsed with members on a schedule; auction machinery to liquidate a defaulter's book; and materially larger regulatory capital held against all of it. Full collateralisation lets you skip every line.

Recommendation

Stay fully collateralised and treat that as a product feature rather than a limitation — it is why you can clear your own trades with a small risk team. Build exact offsetting within risk groups from day one, add cross-instrument portfolio margining in phase two because parlay liquidity depends on it, and treat any proposal to extend credit as a decision to become a different kind of company. Have counsel confirm the specific Part 38 and Part 39 obligations against your final product set; the architecture above does not change either way, but the capital and reporting do.

The ordinary kernel path, since you are going to be on it

The row above retires kernel bypass and points at EFA and DPDK-style drivers, both of which are instance-family dependent and neither of which is guaranteed to cover the gateways. What it does not say is what to do on the plain kernel network path, which is where this venue will actually run. There are two levers there and they solve different problems, which is the part usually got wrong.

  • Busy polling — spinning on a non-blocking receive, or SO_BUSY_POLL on the socket — removes interrupt delivery and thread wake-up from the receive path. This is a tail latency lever, it is the one that moves the NIC to userspace line above, and it fits a design that already budgets roughly six permanently spinning threads (§15). It costs a core and returns p99.
  • io_uring — shared submission and completion rings, with registered buffers, registered files and multishot receive, and with SQPOLL a steady state that submits work without a syscall at all. This is principally a throughput and efficiency lever: it removes per-operation syscall cost, which matters in proportion to how many operations you have.

Those are not interchangeable, and the order in which you reach for them should follow the workload. On an order-entry gateway holding a handful of market-maker connections and already spinning, io_uring adds little — a syscall you are not making is not a cost you are paying, and busy polling has taken the latency already. The place it genuinely earns its keep is the fan-out tier, because the loss of multicast turns one book update into N unicast sends, and that is a syscall-bound workload by construction. It is also, per §13, the component you now own and have to scale. Measure the sends-per-update amplification first; sendmmsg is the cheap move and io_uring with registered buffers is the next one. The retail WebSocket edge is the other natural fit, where connection counts are large and the budget is milliseconds, so nothing is at risk.

Three things to settle before it goes anywhere near production

It does not touch the largest line in the budget. Sequencing and replication at 80–200 µs is Aeron's transport with its own idle strategies, and io_uring does not enter that path without patching a dependency §5 pinned deliberately. Do not let a gateway optimisation be presented as a fix for the replication hop.

SQPOLL costs a core, and its placement is a knob. A kernel poller thread must not land on an isolated core. §15's position is that a host is a triple of image, kernel command line and unit files with nothing tunable at runtime — so that thread's affinity belongs in the triple and in the boot-time profile assertion, not in a tuning session six months later.

The security review is the real gate. io_uring has a substantial CVE history and is disabled or restricted by default in several hardened environments and container-runtime seccomp profiles. Enabling a kernel interface that your own baseline blocks is a decision that needs a written rationale in the change-control record of §15, and a line in the independent system-safeguards testing §19 budgets for, not a default someone sets in a driver flag. If it goes on the fan-out tier, it should be explicitly off everywhere else.

Determinism, at least, is not at risk here, and it is worth recording why rather than leaving it to be rediscovered. Completion order from a ring is not the arrival order a syscall reactor would have produced — but nothing downstream depends on arrival order, because §4 places the ordering decision at the sequencer and the gateway sits outside the determinism boundary. That is what makes the choice a pure performance question. It would stop being one the moment anything inside the boundary started reading from a ring, which is a good reason for the rule to stay written down.

Section 10Carrying a very large instrument count

Volume follows a power law: a few dozen markets carry most of the flow while tens of thousands trade a handful of times a week — and on-demand parlay creation (§2) makes the tail far longer, since most parlays trade exactly once and then sit. Uniform treatment wastes almost all of your compute. Tier by observed activity and let instruments migrate.

Sequencer groups

Group by event ID, not market ID, so every outcome of a categorical event and all its cross-outcome logic stay in one engine. Move an event between sequencer groups with the sequenced transfer of §5, in a scheduled window — never by moving live memory. Size sequencer groups by work rather than by utilisation: the engine's cores busy-spin and always read 100% (§19), so the figure to hold under about 40% at peak is the share of each loop spent processing events. Headroom there is what keeps your p99 honest.

Market data fan-out

Do not create 100 000 multicast groups. Map each market to one of roughly 64–256 channels by sequencer group; market makers subscribe to channels and filter locally, which is what their stacks already do. Retail goes through a separate fan-out tier with per-market WebSocket topics, conflated at 10–20 Hz, backed by an edge-cached snapshot endpoint. A retail client watching one market must never cause work on a matching core.

Market lifecycle

Model it explicitly as a state machine and make every transition a sequenced, audited event: draft → listed → open → (halted) → closed → resolving → settled → archived. The resolution criteria text is part of the contract specification and must be immutable once listed. Editing what a market meant after people have traded it is the fastest way to lose a venue's reputation.

Section 11Clearing, ledger and settlement

The engine is the interesting part; the ledger is the part that gets audited. As your own DCO you are the clearing house, so this section is not a supporting service — it is the regulated core of the business. Build it conservatively.

Ledger

  • Double-entry, append-only, integer cents. No floating point anywhere in the money path. Corrections are compensating entries, never updates.
  • Account tree: customer cash, customer positions, exchange fee income, settlement reserve, and the FBO bank omnibus that backs it all.
  • Continuous invariants, alerted on breach: for every event and every state of it, the payouts owed across all accounts equal the collateral held for the event; no account's available cash is negative; the sum of customer cash equals the FBO balance minus in-flight transfers. A breach halts new listings and pages an operator. Per-book tests such as positions summing to zero do not hold once baskets are minted across an event, which is why the invariant is stated per event and state (§14).
  • Reconcile against the bank daily; reconcile the ledger against a full journal replay nightly.
  • House test accounts for the live smoke suite of §14: company money in accounts flagged as such, reconciled like any other, and excluded from customer balances, segregation calculations and regulatory figures. They touch the FBO omnibus, so compliance signs off on how they are held before the first test dollar moves.

What stays in-house

SMARTS covers wash trading, self-crossing, spoofing and layering, momentum ignition and marking patterns. Three things are specific to event contracts and will not come out of the box:

  • Outcome influence. Participants who can affect the settling event rather than merely predict it. Largely a listing-time control, monitored afterwards as concentration against a small set of flagged event types.

The surveillance problem unique to this venue

In equities, insiders know things. In prediction markets, participants can sometimes cause the outcome. Three defences in order: market design — refuse to list outcomes a small number of participants can control; position limits sized so manipulation costs more than it pays; and monitoring of concentrated pre-resolution positions. Treat the listing committee as a risk control rather than a content function. Most of the damage here is prevented at listing time, not detected afterwards, and no surveillance vendor can do that part for you.

Regulatory reporting

As a DCM and DCO you carry recordkeeping and reporting obligations distinct from surveillance: retained records in a durable, retrievable form; an audit trail capturing order entry through execution with timestamps; and periodic reporting on volume, open interest and large positions. The journal and its archive satisfy the substance of all of these — what needs building is the extraction and formatting, which is a component in §17, not an afterthought. Confirm the specific parts and cadences with counsel against your final product set.

Retain the record in a format you own

A detail with a long tail: write the retained journal in our own SBE encoding, not as Aeron Archive recording segments. Aeron's version sits outside the determinism boundary — a stored journal replays identically whichever release reads it, which is why the engine can be upgraded freely. The archive's on-disk segment format does not sit outside the retention obligation. If the five-year record is a pile of recording segments, then answering a request in 2031 depends on a third party's file layout still being readable twenty-odd releases later, and on us keeping a runnable reader for it. Encode the record in a schema we version alongside the engine, and the dependency is a file in our own repository. Aeron Archive is then operational recovery, which is what it is good at, and the SBE journal is the record.

How long records are kept

Retention periods are set by regulation, not chosen per store. Regulation 1.31 is the base: a regulatory record is kept for at least five years, electronic records stay readily accessible for the whole period (the two-year window applies only to paper), corrections and amendments are records in their own right, and the Commission can ask for any of it in the form it chooses, promptly. The DCM rules apply that to trade records and investigatory and disciplinary files (Regulation 38.951) and require an audit trail that can reconstruct trading from every order, modification and cancellation, protected from alteration and accidental loss (Regulation 38.552); the DCO rules add cleared transactions, margin methodology and test results, and rules and procedures (Regulation 39.20). A retention period is a minimum: every store that holds a regulatory record either meets it or ships the record to one that does.

Building the adapters

The protocol boundary in §3 means each adapter is an independent component. Two are worth calling out:

  • The FIX engine is a buy, or at least an adopt. The session layer — sequence numbers, resend requests, gap fill, logon and logout state, sequence reset — is fiddly, thoroughly specified and completely solved. Writing it yourself is weeks of work to arrive at parity. Note that mature FIX engines are thinner on the ground in Rust than in Java or C++; because the FIX lane is not your lowest-latency path, running that adapter in another language is a reasonable trade, and the protocol boundary is precisely what makes it possible without contaminating the core.
  • The unicast fan-out tier is now yours to own. With multicast unavailable, market-data distribution is a replicator fleet you build, scale and monitor. Budget for it as a real component rather than a line in the gateway.
  • Certification is mandatory. Ship a conformance environment with a scripted suite every market maker must pass before production. It costs a fortnight and saves an outage.
  • Idempotency everywhere. A client order ID is unique per account within a published retention window — not per session, because a resend after failover arrives on a new session, and not per day, because event markets have no trading day to reset on. A duplicate returns the original outcome rather than a second order, decided in sequenced state (§6). This is what makes sequencer failover invisible (§5).
  • Drop copy as a first-class feed. Firms reconcile against it, and so will your support team.
  • Version the wire format from message one, and never repurpose a field.

Read compatibility is cheap; write compatibility is a claim you cannot keep

Split the surface before promising anything, because the two halves have completely different costs.

  • Read — instrument discovery, order book, prices, trade history, positions. This is the large majority of an integration and it is pure representation mapping: decimals instead of cents, a token id instead of a ticker and side. A second read dialect in the on-chain shape is a few hundred lines at the edge and it lets an existing bot's market-scanning half work unmodified.
  • Write — authentication, signing, order submission. Here the gap is structural and no amount of adapter closes it. There is no wallet, no typed-data domain to sign against, no token transfer and no chain. An order that looks identical on the wire would still mean something different, and a venue that advertises drop-in write compatibility earns a support queue full of people whose signing code cannot work by construction.

So say it precisely in the documentation: read dialects for both shapes, one native write path, and a published field-by-field mapping table for the migration. That is an honest promise you can keep, and it is also the larger half of the work you were being asked to save people.

Two supporting pieces make the migration real rather than nominal. Publish a symbology crosswalk — incumbent identifier to venue ticker, as a downloadable file on a stable URL, refreshed on the listing cycle — because without it every integrator writes the same fuzzy string match against event names and each one gets it subtly wrong. And keep an extension namespace: parlays, scalar strips and the RFQ desk of §2 have no incumbent equivalent, so they live under a clearly separated part of the surface that a compatibility-shaped client can ignore entirely rather than choke on.

Two versions on one hostname

Integrators do not experience a new hostname as a small change. It is a firewall allowlist entry, possibly a pinned certificate, and a change-control ticket on their side rather than yours — which is exactly the friction the compatibility work was meant to remove. So one hostname, versions in the path: api.venue.com/v1/… and api.venue.com/v2/…, routed at the edge, which sits in the services regime of §15 and never on the binary path where a proxy hop is unaffordable.

Path prefixes rather than a versioned media type or a query parameter, for two reasons that are specific to a regulated venue. Your access records have to say what a client asked for without anyone reconstructing content negotiation months later; and when support is handed a complaint, a URL can be pasted into a terminal and reproduced, while an Accept header cannot. Purity loses to the audit trail here.

A version boundary may change representation. It must never change semantics.

This is the rule that keeps two live versions from costing you the determinism argument. Renaming a field, reshaping an error, changing pagination or moving from cents to decimals is representation: it is translated at the edge, before sequencing, and the core never learns that v2 exists. Changing what a price means, what an order type does, how allocation works or when an order rests is semantics — and that is not an API version at all. It is a change to the state machine, gated by the protocol_version carried in the journal (§4), rolled out deploy-dark-then-activate (§15), and quite possibly a rule filing before it is an engineering task.

The failure this prevents is specific. If /v1 and /v2 can express two different matching behaviours at the same time, then a replay of the journal has to know which door each order came through in order to reproduce the book — and the audit trail now has two truths. The journal has exactly one dialect. Every adapter translates to the canonical message at the edge and journals that, never the wire bytes it arrived as. This is what makes the third dialect nearly free, and it is the single most important sentence in this section.

The surface already in production shows both the pattern and the trap

ProphetX's partner API is at /v4/ today — path-versioned, on one host, exactly the shape recommended above, and evidently survivable across three prior revisions. Alongside it sit unversioned routes such as /mm/place-wager. That is the trap worth naming: an endpoint shipped without a version prefix is permanently v1, because there is no way to introduce a second shape without either breaking it or bolting a version onto a path that never had one. Version every route from the first release, including the ones that feel too small or too internal to need it, and including the ones you expect to delete.

The same surface carries a semantic trap in its order fields. ProphetX's fillOrKill strategy is not fill-or-kill in the usual sense: an unmatched order waits up to two seconds before it is cancelled. This venue does not reproduce that window. fillOrKill maps to standard FOK — filled in full at once or rejected, never resting and never waiting — and the mapping table and migration notes say so in those words, because a client whose strategy relied on the wait will see rejections where it used to see fills. The conformance environment is where an integrator should discover that, not production.

Inside a version, the rule is additive only: new fields are optional, nothing is removed, nothing is retyped and nothing is ever repurposed — the same discipline the binary format already carries, applied to the whole surface. State in the documentation that clients must ignore unknown fields, so that adding one is not a breaking change by accident.

Retire versions on an announced clock rather than by surprise. Send Deprecation and Sunset headers on every response from a version with an end date, publish that date, and — the part that actually matters — keep per-account telemetry of deprecated-endpoint use, so that when the date approaches you can telephone the eleven firms still on /v1 instead of guessing who breaks. A venue that sunsets an API quietly discovers who was using it from the incident channel.

Copying an API surface is a legal question before it is a technical one

Reimplementing the structure and naming of another venue's interface is broadly understood to be defensible in the United States after Google v. Oracle (2021), but that decision turned on a fair-use analysis of particular facts and is not a blanket permission. Copy no documentation text, no examples and no schema files verbatim; describe your own surface in your own words; and do not let marketing imply endorsement by, or affiliation with, a venue you are compatible with. Have counsel look at the compatibility claim before it is published, for the same reason §18 flags the licence question — the cost of asking is a morning, and the cost of not asking arrives at the worst possible moment.

Onboarding is a product, not a page of documentation

§13's conformance environment already exists for market makers. Extend it: a public sandbox on the same symbology with resettable state and fake money — which is nothing more than a long-lived environment of §16 on its own domain — the conformance suite published so an integrator can self-test before asking for help, and client libraries in the two or three languages your actual applicants use. A prospective firm that can place its first sandbox order in an afternoon converts at a rate that no amount of fee discounting matches.

Section 14Testing and observability

The practice worth more than the rest combined

Golden replay. Every night, replay a full production journal through the candidate engine build and compare the output stream byte for byte against the result log production archived (§18). Determinism makes this possible; it turns "we think the refactor is safe" into a proof, and it is the reason the determinism constraint in §4 is non-negotiable.

Supporting layers

  • Property tests on the invariants that must never break: every event's payouts in every state equal its collateral, available cash is never negative, books are never crossed, price-time priority holds, no order matches through its limit.
  • Fuzzing the wire decoder — it is the venue's actual attack surface.
  • Synthetic market simulation with modelled MM and retail agents, for capacity work and for testing MM protections under stress.
  • Failover drills on a schedule, in production, announced. A failover path that has never been exercised does not exist.
  • Rebuild drills for every store built from the journal (§18): drop it in staging, rebuild it from the archive and diff it against the original. A store nobody has rebuilt is a store nobody can rebuild.

Money adapters: one contract, tested from both sides

Every payment, banking and KYC provider sits behind one interface defined in the venue's own terms, and that interface is the contract both sides are tested against. It fixes what a provider-agnostic treasury needs to know:

  • integer-cent amounts, and an idempotency key on every instruction;
  • a state machine for each transfer, with every transition an explicit event;
  • errors classed as retryable, terminal, or outcome unknown — a timeout after which the money may or may not have moved, resolved by querying the provider and never by retrying blind;
  • asynchronous events that arrive duplicated, out of order, or late: an ACH return days after the customer was credited, a chargeback weeks after a card deposit.

One side: treasury calls the contract correctly. Wallet and treasury logic run in CI against the fake provider, which produces every outcome in the contract on demand. Alongside example-based tests, property tests drive random sequences of provider events — late returns included — and assert that the invariants of §11 still hold, in particular that customer cash equals the FBO balance minus in-flight transfers.

The other side: every implementation passes one conformance suite. The suite is written against the interface and runs against the fake and against each provider's sandbox through its adapter. Running the same suite against both is what keeps the fake honest; without it, every demo and every CI run passes against a fake that has quietly drifted away from what the providers actually do.

Section 15Deployment, infrastructure and change control

This is not a generic DevOps chapter. Two constraints make deployment here unlike deploying a web service: the binary hash is written into the journal (§4), so the build pipeline is part of the correctness argument; and a replicated state machine cannot have two versions of its logic running at once, so "rolling deploy" is not merely risky — it is incoherent.

Why you cannot roll a deploy through the cluster

Every replica must compute identical state from the identical log. A rolling deploy that leaves node 0 on the new binary and nodes 1 and 2 on the old one produces divergent state from the same events, and the cluster is now asserting something untrue about what happened. The fix is to separate shipping the code from changing the behaviour.

Three deployment regimes, deliberately separated

The single biggest structural mistake available here is running one deployment model across the whole system. Two regimes are not enough either: a split in two puts a component budgeted in single-digit microseconds in the same regime as the reporting console. You cannot roll back state

If a bad build wrote events into the journal, reverting the binary does not unwrite them. Rollback means running the previous binary over a log that now contains events it did not produce — which only works if the old binary can still replay them. Two rules follow. Keep a soak period between deploy and activation, during which retreat costs nothing because behaviour has not changed. And when you do activate, verify that the previous binary can still replay past the activation point before you consider the change reversible; if it cannot, the change is one-way and should be treated as such in the approval.

What runs the core, and what does not

The question this section did not previously answer is what actually starts these processes. The default assumption most platform engineers arrive with is Kubernetes, and for the third column that is right. For the first two it is not, and the reasoning belongs on the record so the decision is not relitigated every time the team grows.

The distinction that keeps this from being dogma is between the container and the orchestrator. A container is namespaces and cgroups; with no CPU limit and host networking its steady-state overhead is close to nil. It is not a virtual machine. Nearly everything attributed to containers adding latency is really the orchestrator — its resident daemons, its cgroup limits, its scheduler. Containers are nearly free. Kubernetes is not.

The case in favour is real and worth stating plainly: one toolchain, one runbook shape and a hiring pool that already knows it, against bespoke fleet tooling that tends to live in one engineer's head. That case wins outright for the services column. It loses for the other two, where it trades operational familiarity for latency and for a wider regulated change surface — an upgrade treadmill, a CNI, admission controllers, their own CVE stream, and a shared multi-tenant scheduler that is a harder story to tell an auditor than a fleet of single-purpose machines. It also does not reduce the number of platforms you operate, because the core stays on metal either way.

Containers as an artefact format, not as a runtime

Build the core binaries into OCI images and then never orchestrate them. You get a registry, signing, provenance and hash-addressable artefacts — which plug straight into the reproducible-build gate below and the binary hash written into the journal in §4. Ship the image, extract it into the machine image, run it under supervision on the host. This is the half of the container story that is pure benefit at this latency, and it is available without adopting the half that is not.

Without a reconciliation loop, drift is silent

This is the one thing Kubernetes was genuinely providing, and dropping it has a cost. A node that boots with a different isolcpus mask than its peers is neither corrected nor reported, and the asymmetry surfaces after a failover — which is to say during an incident, the worst available moment to discover it. Build the replacement in phase 0: a preflight that reads back the actual firmware settings, cmdline, IRQ map and hugepage reservation, hashes them, compares against a signed expected profile, and refuses to join consensus on mismatch. The same preflight confirms the PTP hardware clock is present and that ClockBound reports an error bound inside the venue's tolerance before the node joins: a node that cannot say what time it is does not get to sequence anything. It is roughly a day of work and it converts the largest cost of running without an orchestrator into a startup error. Record that profile hash in the deployment record beside the binary hash — not for determinism, since latency is not part of the replay contract, but because “which host profile did Tuesday run under” is the question behind every latency regression you will be asked to explain.

The same signed profile carries the environment's money mode — fake, sandbox or live (§16) — and the same refusal applies to it. A payment, banking or KYC adapter reads back its endpoint host, the mode of its credential and the account it is running in, and will not start if any of them disagrees with the declaration. That turns the most expensive configuration mistake a venue can make, a demo pointed at real money or production pointed at a fake, into a failed boot.

One knob has a counterintuitive answer. Busy-spin idle strategies consume a core permanently, and that is correct here — which makes counting spinning threads a sizing activity rather than a tuning afterthought. Media driver sender, receiver and conductor, consensus module, service container, engine: roughly six threads at full occupancy forever, plus housekeeping cores that must not be shared with them. Instance size falls out of that count, and it has to be settled before the capacity reservations below are placed, because a reservation you are already holding cannot be resized.

ArgoCD is the right tool for the ephemeral tier. This does not reopen §15. Kubernetes was rejected for the production core on two grounds: latency, and a rolling updater let loose on a multi-member consensus group. A single-member cluster has no latency to protect and no quorum to diverge. Two of §15's settings still carry over, because the failure is confusing even in test: the core runs with an OnDelete update strategy, and without a liveness probe, since a probe that kills the only member in the middle of a snapshot looks exactly like a defect in the engine.

Environments are cheap and forgotten environments are not. A mini venue is roughly four vCPU and ten gigabytes including its services, single-node PostgreSQL, ClickHouse and Valkey, and bots, so fifty live ones is a modest line against §19's budget. Fifty abandoned ones, renewed indefinitely, is how dev accounts end up costing more than production. Default to a 72-hour time-to-live, extended by a one-line command, with a janitor that deletes on expiry rather than warning.

Pointing the apps at an environment

Every environment serves a discovery document at /.well-known/venue-env.json: the REST base, the WebSocket and market-data endpoints, the identity issuer, feature flags, and a display name and colour. Apps are built knowing exactly one of those URLs — production's — and everything else is read at runtime. Environment is then a hostname dimension and API version stays a path dimension (§13), so api.pr-1234.venue-dev.com/v4/… composes without either leaking into the other.

  • Web. The bundle deployed into each environment reads the manifest from its own origin. One build serves every environment; nothing is baked in at build time, which is also what lets the preview for a pull request be the artefact that later ships.
  • Mobile. A separate internal flavour — its own bundle identifier, distributed through the internal testing tracks of both stores — carries an environment switcher: choose from the list of live environments, paste a manifest URL, or scan the QR code each environment's landing page shows. Switching clears credentials and local caches, and a banner in the environment's colour stays on screen for as long as the app is pointed anywhere but production.
  • Laptop. The same manifest, pointed at the machine's LAN address so a physical phone can reach it, with certificates from a local development CA that is trusted by the internal flavour and by nothing else. Emulators reach the host through their own loopback alias and need no special case.
An environment switcher in a customer's app is a phishing kit

In a money app, the ability to repoint the backend by opening a link is the ability for an attacker to send a real customer a link that sends their real credentials to a look-alike server. So the switcher is compiled out of the production flavour — not hidden behind a flag, a debug menu or seven taps on the version number, all of which get found. The production app pins its certificates and its manifest URL, and no deep link, push payload or remote configuration value can change which backend it talks to.

Ephemeral environments belong on a different registrable domain

A host like pr-1234.dev.venue.com receives cookies scoped to the production parent domain, and it can set them. A careless or compromised preview environment then becomes a way to read or overwrite production sessions. Put every non-production environment under a separate registrable domain, with its own wildcard DNS and wildcard certificate. The current sandbox already does exactly this — api.sandbox.prophetx.dev, not a subdomain of the production domain — and it should stay that way. Give each environment its own identity issuer as well, so a token minted in one is rejected by every other.

Nothing real is reachable. Not the licensed data feed, whose terms almost certainly forbid redistribution into test systems and whose cost is in §19, and not real money. A bug report is a journal file

Every environment journals exactly as production does. Whoever finds a defect attaches the journal and the scenario seed; whoever fixes it replays that file on a laptop and gets the same outputs byte for byte. Because the history and live-state stores are rebuilt from the journal (§18), the replay reproduces what the app showed as well. One adjustment is needed: an arm64 laptop build carries a different binary hash from the x86 production build, so the replay tool compares outputs rather than insisting the hashes match, and records both. The integer-only engine and the bans of §4 are what make cross-architecture output identical in the first place; build with the same profile, since overflow checks differ between debug and release.

Production data does not come down

Replaying a real journal in a developer's environment puts customer identities and trading records somewhere outside compliance scope, access control and retention policy. The self-serve default is synthetic scenarios only. Where a production-shaped replay is genuinely needed, it comes from a controlled pipeline that replaces account identifiers deterministically — so linkage is preserved and identity is not — with compliance sign-off per extract. Note that a trading pattern can identify a firm on its own; anonymising the identifier is necessary, not sufficient.

Money in every environment

Two requirements pull against each other. A demo environment must be unable to touch real money, and the code that moves real money must be tested against the real providers as thoroughly as they allow. They stop conflicting once the thing being separated is the kind of money rather than the provider: a provider's sandbox is not live money, so reaching one from the right place is not a breach.   • Network. Outbound traffic is denied by default in every account. Dev permits no provider host, cert and staging only the sandbox hosts, prod only the live ones.

Some providers use one host for sandbox and live

Several payment providers serve test and live traffic from the same API host and tell them apart only by the key — Stripe is the best-known example. For those, the network lock cannot separate sandbox from live at all, and the credential and startup locks carry the whole weight. Read the credential's mode at startup and compare it with the declared money mode; never infer the mode from the URL.

Demo balances come from a fake provider, never a ledger shortcut. The fake implements the same interface as the real providers and adds a faucet: a demo user tops up $100, and the fake emits the same webhooks and statement files a provider would. The money then takes its production path — provider event, treasury, ledger posting, sequenced sleeve grant — and daily reconciliation (§11) runs against the fake's statements exactly as it runs against the bank's. A direct balance write would be quicker to build, would skip the code paths a demo exists to exercise, and is precisely the kind of tool that eventually turns out to be reachable in production.

Settling on demand

A tester should not have to wait for a game to finish to see a payout, and an integrator should not have to wait for a postponement to see a void. The dummy oracle lets anyone with access to an environment settle any event in it immediately, and it does so along the path a real result takes — because a settlement that skipped proposal, sign-off and sequencing would test none of the parts that go wrong.

One control API, four ways to drive it. The dummy oracle takes commands per event — start, suspend, resume, resolve with an outcome per market, correct a result, postpone, abandon, go stale — and a schedule that fires them against the sequencer clock. The same commands are reachable from a CLI (venue oracle resolve EVT-8812 --outcome home), from buttons beside each event on the environment's landing page, from a debug panel in the internal app flavour, and from the scenario player, which is that API plus a seed. The panel is compiled out of the production flavour, for the same reason the environment switcher is.

A resolve command takes the production path. It becomes a proposal on the oracle interface of §14, naming the dummy oracle as its source. In a fake-oracle environment an auto-approver signs it: an ordinary client of the console's API holding two test-operator credentials from the environment's own identity issuer, not a flag that skips approval. The resolution is then sequenced, journalled and settled exactly as in production, so the ledger postings, parlay early expiry and the customer's notifications all come from the real code, and the journal attached to a bug report contains the resolution that caused it. Anyone who wants to exercise sign-off and disputes by hand turns the auto-approver off for their environment and works the console as operators do.

The dispute window stays in, and gets short. It is a listing parameter, and the listing templates used by environments set it low. Like every timer after the sequencer it runs on sequencer time (§4), so the compressed clock shortens it again: at the rate that plays a three-hour game in four minutes, a one-hour window closes in about eighty seconds. Nothing about settlement knows it is in a test.

A fake result is as dangerous as a fake deposit

A dummy proposal that settled a real market would pay real customers on an outcome that never happened, and after the dispute window it cannot be quietly reversed. So oracle mode follows money mode: fake in dev, ephemeral and laptop; vendor test or replay feeds in cert and staging, where the licence allows them; live feeds only in prod. The same locks enforce it. The account fixes the mode, egress to vendor hosts is denied everywhere else, vendor credentials exist only where their mode belongs, and every oracle adapter asserts at start, in both directions, that its kind of source matches the declared mode. Resolution adds a fifth lock of its own: a proposal names its source, and the console refuses any source not in the market's registry (§11). The dummy oracle cannot be registered on a production listing, and the auto-approver's credentials come from an issuer production does not trust.

Matching, clearing and risk are one process, not three

What the drawing forbids

Two doors in, both at the same place. Exactly two kinds of arrow enter a sequencer group: client orders through a gateway on 7, and service commands on 14. Both arrive at cluster ingress and both are sequenced. No service calls the engine, nothing but the leader writes the journal, and a new integration that seems to need a third door is a design review, not a ticket.

One record out. Every downstream system reads the same journal on 12. None taps the hot path, holds a connection to the engine or keeps a private copy of its state — which is why the ledger, surveillance and reporting can each be rebuilt from zero and are required to agree with one another.

Readers do not write back. There is no arrow from a reader into the core. A reader that needs to change state does what treasury does and issues a command on 14, so that the change is itself in the journal and replays with everything else.

Interface specifications

Every interface has one specification file, kept with the blueprint in its repository under interfaces/, beside a catalogue (catalogue.toml) from which both of this section's interface tables are generated; a check fails if the blueprint and the catalogue drift. The format follows what the interface is: SBE for the journal and anything binary, OpenAPI 3.1 and AsyncAPI 3 for REST and WebSocket, Rust traits with conformance suites for the payment and oracle contracts, and DDL or a key layout for the stores that more than one component reads. A store owned by one service is not an interface; a table two components depend on is.

The work runs in seven steps: (1) shared types, (2) the journal schema, (3) the reader contract, (4) service commands and the outbox, (5) the contracts in the venue's own terms, (6) the native REST and WebSocket API with the environment manifest, and (7) retention exports and the hold register. The first two exist as a draft schema that the SBE tool validates and that generated Rust code round-trips in tests. Market-maker wire formats are drafted but not frozen until firms have reviewed them, and the interfaces owned by providers, the data vendor, Nasdaq and the CFTC are listed but not specified here.

The journal holds inputs; the result log holds what they did

The journal records what the engine was told. Readers need what it did — fills, cancels, payouts, book levels — and must never re-implement clearing to find out. So each sequencer group also has a result log: the journal and replay nodes run the engine over the committed journal, archive every result beside the journal it came from, and compare a rolling hash with the leader. Each result names the sequence number of its input and its position among that input's results, so a reader's watermark is always an input sequence, and replaying a journal regenerates its result log exactly. Fills state their cash movements and the pairs they minted and burned outright for the same reason. Every journal input begins with a fixed record header that a gateway sends zeroed and the sequencer stamps in place, so the bytes a client's order arrives as are the bytes the journal keeps.

Where data lives

The deterministic core has no database, on purpose: a query inside the engine would break §4 and a write would break §9's budget. Books, positions and sleeves live in memory, the journal is the record, and recovery is snapshot plus replay. Everything outside the core does need stores, and the rule for choosing one is what the data does rather than what shape it has. Small records that change go in PostgreSQL; large immutable events go in ClickHouse; the live state apps ask for constantly is held in Valkey by a reader of the journal.

Use one PostgreSQL per domain rather than one shared database. The ledger gets its own, with the strictest backup, point-in-time restore and retention settings; accounts, market operations and treasury get theirs. A shared database is a shared outage and a shared schema migration.

Four rules make the stores safe to rely on.

  • Nothing the engine needs is read from a store. A listing, resolution, sleeve grant or rule limit kept in PostgreSQL reaches a sequencer group as a command on 14, and the engine knows only the command (§4).
  • Services reach a sequencer group through an outbox. Treasury writes the instruction and its idempotency key in the same transaction as its own records; a relay sends the command; and the instruction is marked done only when the relay sees the event in the journal. Writing to the database, then sending and hoping, is how a sleeve grant goes missing.
  • Every journal reader applies each event exactly once and knows how far it has got. The mechanism differs because the stores do. A PostgreSQL reader stores the last applied sequence per group in the same transaction as its rows. The ClickHouse loader cuts batches at fixed sequence boundaries and inserts each with a deduplication token derived from its range, so a retried batch is dropped rather than doubled, and it publishes its watermark only once the insert is acknowledged. The Valkey projection applies a batch in one atomic script per account, checking and advancing that account's sequence. History tables hold events and are never updated: an order's status is the latest of its event rows.
  • Reads can wait for their own writes. Every order acknowledgement carries its sequence number. A request that must include it states that sequence as a minimum and waits briefly for the watermark to reach it, rather than showing a customer a history without the order they just placed.

Serving the apps. Open orders, positions and recent fills come from Valkey, which keeps them for every account rather than guessing whose will be asked for. Open orders are bounded by the slab of §6, so the whole projection is small, and it holds nothing authoritative and none of §12's rate-limit counters. A WebSocket connection starts with a snapshot at watermark N and streams from N+1, which is gap-free by construction. Older history comes from ClickHouse through the API service, never directly: a page that lies wholly before a sequence number can never change, so it is cached indefinitely; history queries run under a concurrency cap; and app traffic uses replicas that research and surveillance do not, so an analyst's scan cannot slow a customer's history page. Public price charts use candles precomputed by the loader and cached at the edge (§13).

ClickHouse suits a few heavy queries, and apps send many small ones

This is the one real risk in the storage design. A per-account lookup on a table ordered by account and time is fast, but thousands of small concurrent queries are not ClickHouse's natural load. Valkey takes the hottest reads and immutable pages are cached, which leaves long-tail history browsing. Load-test that at launch user numbers before depending on it, and confirm on the chosen deployment that an acknowledged insert is visible to the next read, because the watermark rule depends on it (§21).

Two licence questions worth settling early

Several standard infrastructure tools — Terraform and Packer among them — moved from an open-source licence to the Business Source Licence in 2023, and community forks now exist under permissive terms. The restriction is aimed at competing offerings rather than at users, so an exchange is very unlikely to be affected, but tooling that sits in a regulated change-control chain is exactly where you want a written answer rather than an assumption. The same applies to anything copyleft in the observability stack if you ever modify and expose it. Neither is a blocker; both are cheap now and awkward to unpick during an examination.

The data vendor is a settlement dependency, and therefore a regulatory one

Resolution for sports contracts rests on a commercial data feed, and for several leagues the official data rights are exclusive — which means that for some of your most liquid markets there is effectively one source. That has three consequences. The contract specification must name the source and state what happens when it is wrong, late or unavailable. The vendor becomes a manipulation surface in its own right, the same observation §22 makes about a perpetual's index, and §12 has to treat it as one. And the two-of-three human sign-off in §17 is not ceremony — it is the control that exists because the feed is single-sourced.

Grouping follows the event, so every Chiefs–Bills market co-locates in P1 regardless of shape; season-long futures sit in P2; parlays have P3 to themselves. The parlay's legs therefore live in P1 and P2 while the parlay itself lives in P3 — and that is fine, because a parlay is self-collateralising. The buyer pays the premium, the seller posts $1 minus the premium, and the worst case is fully funded without reference to the legs. No risk group spanning sequencer groups is required. Offsetting the parlay against a position in its own legs is portfolio margining, which is exactly why the risk group calculator is a phase-2 line in §17 rather than a launch dependency.

Follow the sell. The gateway decodes the binary message, authenticates the session, resolves the instrument and rejects anything malformed — total validation before the boundary, per §5's poison-pill defence. It publishes to P1, where the consensus module assigns the sequence, writes the log, and replicates. Only on a majority does the clustered service receive the callback, and it does nothing but forward the committed event over shared memory to the engine.

The engine checks collateral against the account's P1 sleeve: 500 uncovered at a 38¢ worst case is $190, and if the sleeve is short the order is rejected here rather than anywhere upstream. Two hundred contracts cross against a resting retail bid at 62¢. Because the seller is going short from flat, this is a mint, not a transfer — $124 from the buyer and $76 from the seller lock 200 complete sets at $1, the buyer takes 200 YES and the seller 200 NO. Three hundred rest on the book. The delta goes to the fan-out tier; the execution report goes back through the service container and the gateway.

Then the Cowboys lose

At four o'clock a leg of the parlay resolves false in P1. The leg-to-parlay index of §2 finds every parlay referencing it, and P3 sequences an expiry: the contract is worth zero, and the seller's collateral is released in September rather than in January when the playoff leg would finally have resolved. That is the commercial argument for the index made concrete — four months of a market maker's capital, returned on a Sunday afternoon, on an instrument that had no reason to stay open.

The Chiefs contract resolves the other way. The data vendor proposes, two of three operators sign off, and a resolution event enters P1's log. The engine terminates the book at $1 and $0, cancels resting orders, and collapses positions: the buyer's 200 YES become $200 — their own $124 plus the seller's $76 — and the seller's NO is worthless. Settlement posts to the ledger on the services tier. Every step of it is in the journal, and replays.

That is the venue on machines, running a Sunday. What it has not said is what any of it costs, which is the subject of §19 — and the answer is that the twenty metal instances above are the smallest line in the budget.

Every other budget in this document is denominated in microseconds. This one is in dollars: what §18's twenty metal instances cost, and the four larger expenses of running a venue that do not appear in the component inventory at all.

A DCO holds financial resources under Part 39 against two things: the default of its largest member, and a year of its own operating costs. Full collateralisation (§8) drives the first leg to approximately zero — there is no uncollateralised exposure to cover, so there is no guaranty fund, no skin-in-the-game tranche and no assessment powers to capitalise. What an instrument costs

§10 asserts that the marginal cost of a listed instrument must approach zero. With a budget in hand, that becomes arithmetic rather than aspiration. A dormant instrument is a fixed-size ladder array plus an occupancy bitmap and a little metadata — call it 16 KB resident. Half a million of them is 8 GB, which is a rounding error on machines bought for their cache behaviour rather than their capacity, and they consume no journal bytes at all until someone touches them.

So the marginal instrument costs essentially nothing and the average instrument costs $1.30 a year at half a million listings, falling as you list more. This is the number that decides what kind of venue you are. At $1.30 you list every game in a season, every band of a scalar strip and every candidate in a categorical field, and you let §2's RFQ desk price the tail. At $100 an instrument — which is what you get if a book needs a process, a database row set and a monitoring dashboard — you list two hundred markets, you compete with everyone else listing the same two hundred, and the tiered activity model of §10 was never worth building. The engineering in §6 and §10 is a commercial strategy wearing a data structure.

What has to be true

Against a $10M run-rate, at a penny a side — substitute your own schedule — the venue clears its costs at roughly a billion contracts a year, or something over two million a day. That is a volume statement rather than an engineering one, and it is worth writing on the same page as the architecture for two reasons.

  • It is the justification for instrument breadth. Two million contracts a day is unreachable from a handful of headline events, which are also the events every competitor lists. It is reachable from a long tail that only exists if listing is nearly free — which is the argument of the paragraph above, arriving from the other direction.
  • It sets the latency requirement honestly. Volume of that shape comes from market makers, and market makers price the §9 budget into their spreads or decline to quote. The microseconds are not an engineering preference. They are the reason the contracts-per-day figure is achievable at all, and that is the sentence to use when the latency work is asked to justify itself against a cheaper design.
The cheapest thing in this document

§4's determinism and §14's replay harness cost a meaningful fraction of the early build and appear on no budget line as a benefit. What they buy is an audit answered by replaying a journal instead of by six weeks of reconstruction, a defect diagnosed from a recorded input sequence instead of reproduced in production, and a regulator's question about a specific trade at a specific microsecond answered the same afternoon. Nothing else in §20's build plan has that return, and it is invisible in every quarter where nothing goes wrong.

Seeding the tail with market maker obligations

The venue does not quote its own book — correctly, since the disclosure and information-barrier machinery it would need is a poor trade against the reputational exposure. The tail gets its two-sided prices from designated market makers under a published obligation programme instead. The commercial insight is that you are buying presence, not tightness: a 15¢-wide two-sided quote in a cold market is a functioning market, and an empty book is not.

Why the tiering in §10 is what makes this affordable

The hard part of quoting the tail is not capital, it is adverse selection: you get hit precisely when news breaks and you have not updated, across thousands of instruments nobody can actively watch. With every book matched continuously, that risk is carried by controls rather than by the market model: the per-event rate-based auto-pull of §6, spreads and sizes that are wide and small by design, and a position cap. Together they make quoting ten thousand cold instruments a bounded, modellable risk instead of an open-ended one. The activity tiering and the market maker programme were designed separately and turn out to carry each other — make that argument explicitly when you negotiate the first obligations.

Measurement and enforcement

Compliance is computed from the journal — presence, spread and restoration time per instrument per session, derived from the same book delta stream that feeds market data. That matters for two reasons: the numbers are reproducible, so a disputed month can be replayed and shown rather than argued; and the calculation is a component, not a spreadsheet (§17). Publish a monthly scorecard to each firm, and make consequences graduated and mechanical — a warning, then reduced rebate tier, then loss of designated status.

As a DCM the programme must be rule-based and non-discriminatory: published criteria, open to any firm that qualifies, part of the filed rulebook rather than a private arrangement. This is the structural contrast with quoting your own account — a market maker programme is a normal, well-precedented exchange mechanism, and the transparency that regulation demands of it is also what makes it defensible.

When no firm will quote a market at any price

Sometimes the answer is not to find liquidity but to not list the contract. If no market maker will show a two-sided price at a 20¢ spread with a position cap, that is information about the instrument — usually that its resolution is ambiguous, its timeline is unbounded, or its outcome is influenceable. List fewer, better contracts, and put the genuinely marginal ones in a no-obligation tier, where they trade if and only if two natural participants happen to meet. Manufacturing a price for a contract nobody will quote is how a venue ends up defending a settlement it cannot justify.

Section 22Non-binary payoffs and leverage

A perpetual on something like a team's offence-minus-defence differential bundles two requests with very different price tags. Continuous payoffs instead of 0/1 is nearly free — the primitive is already specified. Leverage is a different company. Unbundling them is most of the decision, and it is worth doing before any engineering.

Three asks, not two

“Leverage” is usually asked for when what is actually wanted is capital efficiency. They are separable, and only the third one changes what kind of business you are:

AskWhat it meansWhat it costs
Non-binary payoffContinuous exposure to an index level or spread, not a 0/1 outcomeSmall. A strip of binaries over adjacent bands — already in the taxonomy (§2) and already scheduled (§21).
Capital efficiencyNot posting full notional on each leg of an offsetting positionModerate, and already planned. Worst-case-state netting across a risk group (§8): in-event releases at the fill from day one, cross-mint advances in phase two (§7).
LeverageCollateral held is less than the worst-case loss, the shortfall extended as creditstructural

The capped linear contract — most of the product, none of the rebuild

A contract paying proportionally to an index between a floor and a cap, clamped at both ends, is economically a call spread and decomposes into exactly the binary strip of §2. Its maximum loss is cap − floor, known at trade time and independent of where the index goes. That means it is fully collateralisable, and every line of §7 and §8 survives untouched: no mark price, no margin model, no liquidation engine, no insurance fund, no waterfall.

The capital-efficiency complaint that usually pushes a venue toward leverage is largely answered by worst-case-state netting — in-event releases from day one and cross-mint advances in phase two (§7). A trader long one band and short an adjacent one has a bounded net exposure, and charging full notional on each leg is simply wrong — the same computation as the categorical scan in §7. That recovers most of what customers experience as leverage, without extending any credit.

What survives if you build real perpetuals, and what does not

The good news is counterintuitive: the parts of this design that were hardest to get right are the parts that survive. A perpetual on a bounded index still has a finite tick ladder — at 0.1-point ticks across a ±30 range that is 600 levels, and §6 already carries 999-level books for parlays on a two-level bitmap. The array-and-bitmap layout is unchanged. Determinism survives too: mark price and funding rates arrive as sequenced events under the rule in §4, and liquidation triggers fire off the same tick events that drive expiry.

What breaks is everything underneath the engine:

TodayUnder leverage
Position is one signed integer; complete sets collapse to $1 (§7)Needs entry price, cost basis, unrealised PnL and continuous mark-to-market. The ledger roughly doubles.
Pre-trade check is uncovered × price ≤ cash — O(1) and exactNeeds a margin computation, and margin depends on a mark that moves independently of the order.

The sleeve row is the subtle one and it has an architectural answer. Sleeves work today because loss is bounded at trade time, so a grant stays good until positions change. Under leverage an account's requirement moves when the market moves, and a cross-group margin call cannot be made synchronously. The clean resolution is the move already made for parlays: perpetuals get their own sequencer group, with a single risk core rather than distributed sleeves. §7 rejected a single risk core because it recouples the sequencer groups; for a product whose volume will sit far below the binary books and whose risk coupling is far tighter, that trade comes out the other way.

The index is the hard part, not the engine

This is where the specific example gets into trouble, and the problem is not solvable with better software. An NFL offence-minus-defence differential updates once a week per team, discretely, with essentially all information arriving inside a three-hour window on a Sunday. From Monday to Saturday the index does not move at all.

The perpetual form is solving a problem this underlying does not have

Perpetuals exist to give continuous exposure to a continuously traded thing without expiry rollover, and the funding rate is what tethers the contract to spot. Against an index that steps once a week, funding spends six days tethering the contract to a value everyone already knows is stale, and the liquidation engine has no price path to work with. A weekly-stepping index wants a series of weekly-settling dated contracts — which settle cleanly against the published statistic, need no liquidation engine at all, and can be fully collateralised. If the product goal is exposure to a season-long statistic, dated contracts express it better than a perpetual does, and cost a fraction as much to run.

There is a surveillance dimension too. §12 identifies outcome influence as this venue's signature manipulation risk. Leverage multiplies the payoff to influencing the index, and a perpetual removes the resolution date that surveillance would otherwise concentrate around. The index would also come from a data vendor, making that vendor a single point of both failure and manipulation in a way no binary contract's resolution source currently is.

If it gets built anyway

Written down so the decision is made with open eyes. Each of these is a component in the sense of §17, and several are teams rather than sprints:

  • Mark price service — index ingestion, a manipulation-resistant construction, published and sequenced into the journal so that every liquidation is replayable.
  • Funding rate calculator, computed on a fixed cadence and sequenced like any other parameter change.
  • Margin engine — initial and maintenance requirements, per position and per portfolio, with the model validation, back-testing and stress testing that §8 lists.
  • Liquidation engine with partial liquidation, a queue, and a defined backstop for the case where the book cannot absorb the position.
  • Insurance fund with a published balance, sized against the gap rather than against ordinary volatility.
  • Default waterfall and assessment powers, plus the member capital and admission standards that make assessments meaningful.

Note what is not on that list: automatic deleveraging. It is the usual last resort offshore, and it works by tearing up a winning participant's position to cover a loss elsewhere. On a DCM that is very hard to defend, and it should not be the mechanism you plan to rely on.

The regulatory gate

Two separate hurdles, and both are decided by counsel rather than by architecture. On the contract: the Commission's June 2026 proposed rulemaking on prediction markets is broadly receptive to sports contracts, but identifies as likely contrary to the public interest those settling on player injuries, officiating decisions, or a discrete action or occurrence within a game. A season-aggregate statistical differential is plausibly on the right side of that line precisely because it is an aggregate rather than a discrete occurrence — but that is a proposed rule in an area that has moved repeatedly, and the reading needs confirming rather than assuming.

On leverage: clearing a margined product engages Part 39 in earnest — a margin methodology under Regulation 39.13 with daily review, back-testing and stress testing, and financial resources composed of the defaulting member's margin, your own capital, guaranty fund deposits, default insurance and assessment powers. Full collateralisation currently makes that section nearly vacuous for you. Leverage converts it into a standing capital and model-validation obligation, which is the same conclusion §8 reaches by a different route.

Recommendation

Ship capped linear contracts built from the scalar strip, with worst-case-state margining, and let the market say whether what is missing is genuinely leverage or merely capital efficiency — because if it is the latter, portfolio margining already answers it. That is a phase-three product on the existing architecture rather than a new one.

If leveraged perpetuals are still wanted afterwards, treat them as a separate regulated product line with their own sequencer group, their own risk core, their own insurance fund and their own capital — and pick an underlying that actually trades continuously. A weekly statistic does not, and no amount of engineering makes a discontinuous index safe to liquidate against.
